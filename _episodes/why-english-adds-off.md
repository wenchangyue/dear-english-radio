## Words and phrases

**particle** — A small word added to a verb, such as OFF in TAKE OFF.

**relation** — The way two things are connected.

**reference point** — The place or thing used to understand a position.

**separation** — Moving or being apart.

**departure** — Leaving a place.

**Old English** — An early form of English used many centuries ago.

**take off** — Remove something, leave, rise from the ground, or become successful quickly; the sentence chooses the meaning.

**turn off** — Stop a device or supply from operating.

**cut off** — Separate something or suddenly stop a connection or supply.

**call off** — Cancel a planned event or activity.

**put off** — Delay something until a later time.

**break off** — Separate a part or end an activity, talk, or relationship.

**time off** — Time away from usual work.

**go off** — Sound, explode, stop operating, or leave; the situation chooses the meaning.

**pull off** — Succeed at doing something difficult or unexpected.

**interrupt** — Stop something before it normally ends.

**negotiations** — Formal talks intended to reach an agreement.

**dialect** — A form of a language used by a region or group.

**predictable** — Possible to guess from a known pattern.

**separable** — Allowing an object between the verb and particle.

**pronoun** — A word such as IT or THEM that replaces a noun.

**whole expression** — A group of words learned as one complete meaning.

## 2. One Cable, One Picture

**Eli:** On our desk, a cable is plugged into the recorder. Now I pull the plug out.

**Nora:** The cable is unplugged. It is no longer connected to the recorder.

**Eli:** That gives us a starting picture: connected, then separated. English `off` has long carried the idea of “away” or “away from.”

**Nora:** Here is a short piece of word history. Around the year 1200, `off` appeared as a stronger form of the Old English word `of`. That older word meant “away” or “away from.”

**Eli:** So `off` and `of` were once much closer than they are today.

**Nora:** Yes. Over time, the two words took different jobs. The meaning “not working” came much later. It is recorded from 1861.

**Eli:** That history helps us see why “away” is a good starting point. But it cannot explain every modern phrase.

**Nora:** We’ll use the old idea in a modern learning map. We are not claiming that every phrase came from one simple story.

**Eli:** In a clear example, check the main verb first. It may tell you the action.

**Nora:** `Off` may tell you what changes. Something leaves a surface. A person leaves a place. A flow stops. A plan is no longer active.

**Eli:** Take this simple sentence: “I cut the paper.” You know the action, but you do not know the result. Maybe the paper now has a line in it.

**Nora:** Now say, “I cut the corner off.” The corner becomes separated from the rest of the paper. `Cut` gives the method. `Off` gives the result.

**Eli:** This example splits clearly. The base verb already has meaning.

**Nora:** Here, that meaning stays clear. The small particle adds a relation or a result. A `particle` is a small word in a phrasal verb, such as `off` in `take off`.

**Eli:** Other combinations do not split so neatly. `Call off`, `put off`, and `pull off` have standard meanings that we must check as complete expressions.

**Eli:** Think of a jacket resting on your body. “Take the jacket” only tells me to take it. “Take the jacket off” tells me to remove it from your body.

**Nora:** The jacket starts in contact with a support. It ends separated from that support.

**Eli:** That same scene works with `peel the label off`, `wipe the dust off`, `cut a branch off`, and `break a piece off`.

**Eli:** We can test the picture without a phrasal verb. Put a coffee cup on the table.

**Nora:** The cup is supported by the table. Now move it away. The cup is off the table.

**Nora:** English can stretch the same relation into distance. A station can be two miles off the main road. A deadline can still be a long way off.

**Eli:** We can also move away from an expected line. If a conversation goes off topic, it leaves the subject people were discussing.

**Nora:** This gives us a wider idea than “not operating.” `Off` can mark distance from a surface, place, connection, activity, or expected path.

**Eli:** That wider idea explains why translating `off` as one word causes trouble. A translation may work for `turn off the lamp` and fail in `take a day off`.

**Nora:** Start with the relation. Then use the sentence to find the reference point: the place, surface, plan, or connection that something moves away from.

**Eli:** Think of four questions. What was the thing connected to? Was it touching a surface? Was it using a supply? Was it following a plan or path?

**Nora:** You do not need to ask all four every time. These questions train your attention. With enough listening, you will notice the relation more quickly.

**Eli:** Quick test. I say, “A button broke.” What do you know?

**Nora:** The button became damaged. It may still be on the shirt.

**Eli:** I say, “A button broke off.”

**Nora:** Now the button is separated from the shirt.

**Eli:** That is real work from a very small word.

## 3. From Separation to Departure

**Nora:** Now move from an object to a person. You are standing beside a table. Then you walk away from it.

**Eli:** I can say, “Eli walked off.” The starting place may be clear from the scene, even when I do not name it.

**Nora:** The same pattern appears in `drive off`. A car leaves its starting point. In `set off`, a person begins a trip.

**Eli:** And an airplane `takes off`. It leaves contact with the ground.

**Nora:** That picture makes the aircraft sense easy to remember. It does not explain every use of `take off`, though.

**Eli:** Right. “Take your shoes off” means remove them. “The plane took off” means it left the ground. “Her business took off” means it became successful quickly. “I have to take off” is an informal way to say I have to leave.

**Nora:** Those senses belong to one expression. The relation between them can support memory, but context chooses the meaning.

**Eli:** Listen to the noun after the verb. If I take `my coat` off, something is removed. If `the plane` takes off, it departs. If `sales` take off, they rise quickly.

**Nora:** Grammar is part of meaning. A diagram of `off` cannot replace the sentence.

**Eli:** Compare “I took the book off the shelf” with “I took the cover off the book.”

**Nora:** In the first sentence, the book leaves the shelf. In the second, the cover leaves the book.

**Eli:** In both sentences, the noun before `off` is the thing that moves. `Off the shelf` and `off the book` name the earlier place or support.

**Nora:** That is different from a separable phrasal verb such as `turn off the light` or `turn the light off`. We will return to that pattern near the end.

**Eli:** Learners sometimes recognize every word but lose track of who moves away from what.

**Nora:** Build the final picture. The cover ends separated from the book. Once you see that result, the word order becomes easier to follow.

**Eli:** Here is an everyday example. Your meeting ends at five. A coworker asks, “Do you want coffee?” You look at the train schedule and say, “Sorry, I have to take off.”

## 4. When a Connection Stops

**Nora:** “Shut the water off.” The water supply stops.

**Eli:** “The call was cut off.” The connection ended before the speakers wanted it to end.

**Nora:** These complete expressions describe different changes. `Turn off` and `switch off` point to a control. `Shut off` closes a supply. `Cut off` can describe a sudden or unwanted break.

**Eli:** You can feel why `off` belongs here. The system was connected and active. Then the connection or flow was interrupted.

**Nora:** Be careful with the base verb. “I cut the call” is not the normal way to say the connection ended. English uses the combination `cut off`.

**Eli:** This is where many learners get frustrated. A meaning map can show the general direction. A dictionary tells you whether English really uses that verb and particle together.

**Nora:** Two classroom studies in our sources reached different results. One reported better test gains with a particle-based method. In another, a mind-map group did not do better than a memorization group.

**Eli:** So the map helps us group ideas. It is not a proven best method for every learner. Meet each expression in sentences. Say it, hear it, and try to remember it later without looking.

**Nora:** Let’s try three small changes.

**Eli:** Your laptop is running. You press the power control.

**Nora:** You turn it off.

**Eli:** Water is flowing under the sink. You close the valve.

**Nora:** You shut it off.

## 5. A Plan Can Lose Its Connection Too

**Nora:** Physical cables are easy to see. Plans have connections too, but those connections are mental and social.

**Eli:** A meeting is connected to a date. Two companies are connected through negotiations. An employee is connected to the work schedule.

**Nora:** English extends `off` into these situations, but each verb creates a different change.

**Eli:** Start with `call off`. “They called off the meeting.” The planned meeting will not happen. It was canceled.

**Nora:** Now `put off`. “They put off the meeting until Friday.” The meeting may still happen. It was moved away from the original time.

**Eli:** That difference is important. `Call off` removes the event from the active plan. `Put off` moves it later.

**Nora:** Imagine a dentist appointment on Tuesday. You call the office and say, “I need to put off my appointment until next week.” You are asking to delay it.

**Eli:** If the dentist closes the office for the month, the appointment may be called off. It is canceled.

**Nora:** Now consider `break off`. “The two companies broke off negotiations.” The continuing relationship or activity ended.

**Eli:** The physical sense helps. You can break a small piece off a larger object. In the abstract use, an ongoing link between people or activities is ended.

**Nora:** The shared picture helps us organize the meanings used today. It does not prove how every phrase developed in history.

**Eli:** There is also `time off`. “I’m taking Friday off.” You will be away from your usual work duties that day.

**Nora:** And `off duty` means you are not working in your official role at that time.

**Eli:** Mina’s meeting examples now sit in two different places on the map. `Call off` means cancel. `Put off` means delay.

**Nora:** Adults meet these two phrases in emails all the time. Here is one message: “Tomorrow’s interview has been called off.” Should you arrive tomorrow?

**Eli:** No. The interview is canceled. You may need a new plan, but the message does not promise a new date.

**Nora:** Now read: “Tomorrow’s interview has been put off until Monday.”

**Eli:** The interview is still planned. Its connection to tomorrow is gone, and it is now connected to Monday.

**Nora:** The timeline makes the distinction visible. `Put off` moves the event. `Call off` removes it from the current schedule.

**Eli:** What if the email says only, “The interview is off”?

**Nora:** In ordinary context, that usually means it is canceled or no longer happening. The short form does not tell you whether someone will arrange another date later.

**Eli:** And “The interview is still on” means it is still planned to happen.

**Nora:** Here `on` and `off` describe two states. The event is still on the schedule, or it is no longer on the schedule.

**Eli:** That pattern appears outside meetings. A deal can be on or off. A trip can be on or off. The exact situation decides what plan is being discussed.

**Nora:** Now compare work. “I’m off today” means I am not working today. “I’m taking today off” presents the day as time away from my duties.

**Eli:** Both are common ways to talk about work. Neither one is about turning off a machine.

**Nora:** Right. The sentence tells us that a person is away from work.

**Nora:** Let’s make the contrast practical. Your team plans a picnic for Saturday. Heavy rain is coming, and the team decides there will be no picnic.

**Eli:** They call it off.

**Nora:** Notice the pronoun: `call it off`.

**Eli:** Now the team moves the picnic to Sunday.

**Nora:** They put it off until Sunday.

**Eli:** One event disappears from the plan. The other moves along the timeline.

## 6. The Alarm Paradox

**Eli:** We are ready for the alarm.

**Nora:** At six o’clock, my alarm went off. It began making a loud sound. This use does not follow the “stopped connection” picture.

**Eli:** `Go off` has several dictionary senses.

**Nora:** Historical records show `go off` used for a gun firing or an explosion in the 1570s. Modern dictionaries also list an alarm ringing, a light stopping, and a person leaving.

**Eli:** Some of these uses may feel related. But our sources do not show that the alarm meaning came from the gun meaning. It is safer to learn them as meanings of the whole expression.

**Nora:** In another context, “The lights went off” means the lights stopped operating. In “She went off to work,” it means she left.

**Eli:** The word pair is the same. The subject and situation select the stored sense.

**Nora:** An alarm is a warning device, so “went off” normally means it sounded. Lights are operating devices, so “went off” normally means they stopped.

**Eli:** So `off` does not secretly mean `on`. The whole expression has more than one standard meaning.

**Nora:** Speakers learn those standard meanings from hearing the phrase in different situations.

**Eli:** Here is a safe learning habit. When one phrase seems to break your map, do not throw away the map. Check the whole entry.

**Nora:** Look at the subject. Look at what happens next. Check whether the dictionary marks a meaning as British, American, formal, or informal.

**Eli:** For example, in British English, `food has gone off` means it has become bad. The learner dictionaries we checked mark this use as British.

**Nora:** Context and dialect matter. A one-word translation hides both.

**Eli:** Now solve four lines.

**Nora:** “At midnight, every light in the building went off.”

**Eli:** They stopped operating.

**Nora:** “At midnight, the fire alarm went off.”

**Eli:** It started making a warning sound.

**Nora:** “The fireworks went off over the river.”

**Eli:** They exploded.

## 8. The Five-Second OFF Test

**Eli:** Let’s turn the whole episode into a five-second test.

**Nora:** First, check the base verb. In a clear example, what action does it help you see?

**Eli:** Second, find the possible connection. Is something on a surface, attached to a support, using a supply, linked to a plan, or moving from a starting point?

**Nora:** Third, picture the end. Is it separated, gone, inactive, canceled, or delayed?

**Eli:** Fourth, check the full situation. The subject, object, and dialect help you choose the right meaning.

**Nora:** Fifth, check the grammar. With a separable phrasal verb, a personal-pronoun object such as `it` or `them` must go between the verb and particle: `turn it off`, `call it off`, `put it off`, `pull it off`.

**Eli:** Not `turn off it`.

**Nora:** Right. A noun often has more flexibility. You may hear “turn off the light” or “turn the light off.” With `it`, use “turn it off.”

**Eli:** Final round. Five situations. Give yourself two seconds for each.

**Nora:** One. A sticker is still on a glass jar. You remove it with your fingers.

**Eli:** You peel it off. Physical separation.

**Nora:** Two. A driver leaves quickly after dropping you at the station.

**Eli:** The driver drove off. Departure.

**Nora:** Three. Your manager cancels tomorrow’s meeting.

**Eli:** She calls it off. The meeting will not happen.

**Nora:** Four. She moves the meeting to next week.

**Eli:** She puts it off until next week. The event is delayed.

**Nora:** Five. Your small team completes a project that everyone thought was too difficult.

**Eli:** You pulled it off. Learn this meaning with the whole expression.

**Nora:** And our opening question: Why does an alarm go off when it starts making noise?

**Eli:** Because dictionaries give the whole expression a specific alarm sense: it sounds. The alarm context selects that meaning. `Off` itself has not changed into `on`.

**Nora:** Mina wanted a system. Start with “away” or “separated.” Then look for departure, a stopped connection, a canceled plan, or distance in time. The full expression and context give you the final meaning.
